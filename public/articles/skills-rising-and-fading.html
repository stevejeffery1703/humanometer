The future of work · data, not hype

The skills rising fastest in the AI era (and the ones fading)

Nearly two-fifths of the skills you use at work today will be obsolete or transformed by 2030. That sounds alarming until you look at what's actually rising to take their place — and notice that the most durable winners aren't technical at all.

By Humanometer staff· August 2026· 7 min read

Every few years the World Economic Forum surveys thousands of the world's largest employers and asks a blunt question: which skills are you hiring for, and which are you letting go? Its Future of Jobs Report 2025 is the closest thing we have to a live readout of where the labor market is heading. One number frames the whole thing:

39%
of workers' core skills will be transformed or outdated by 2030
World Economic Forum, Future of Jobs Report 2025

The instinct is to panic and go learn to code. That's not wrong, exactly — but it misreads the data. Demand isn't shifting in one direction. It's splitting three ways.

Rising, part one: the technical skills

The single fastest-growing skill in the world right now is exactly what you'd guess.

90%
of employers expect increased demand for AI and big-data skills — the #1 fastest-growing skill through 2030
WEF Future of Jobs Report 2025

Right behind it: cybersecurity and networks, and general technological literacy. If you have the appetite for it, these are real, well-paid, growing lanes. But there's a catch worth being honest about — this is the part of the market where you're competing most directly with the technology itself. The specific tool you learn today may be automated, abstracted away, or obsolete in five years. Technical skill is a rising escalator you have to keep climbing.

Rising, part two: the durable human skills

Here's the part the "just learn to code" take misses entirely. Alongside the technical skills, employers rank a cluster of distinctly human capabilities as core — and growing.

#1
Analytical thinking is the most-demanded core skill of all — considered essential by 7 in 10 employers
WEF Future of Jobs Report 2025

The WEF's rising list reads like a description of good human judgment:

  • Analytical thinking — the top core skill, full stop
  • Creative thinking — among the fastest-growing skills overall
  • Resilience, flexibility and agility — thriving through constant change
  • Curiosity and lifelong learning — the meta-skill of staying current
  • Leadership and social influence — reading and moving people

Unlike a specific technical tool, these don't expire when the next model ships. They're the skills that let you use whatever tool arrives next. They compound rather than decay — which makes them the smartest long-term bet on the list.

Fading: the routine and the manual

So what's actually declining? Overwhelmingly, it's work that is repetitive, rule-bound, or purely physical-routine.

24%
of employers foresee falling demand for manual dexterity, endurance and precision — the standout decline
WEF Future of Jobs Report 2025

Clerical and administrative roles face the steepest job decline, and the skills tied to routine data-handling and repetitive processing are fading with them. The common thread: if a task is well-defined and predictable, it's exactly what software is best at absorbing. The value drains out of doing the routine and pools around judging, deciding, and handling the exceptions.

The pattern under the lists

Strip away the specifics and one rule explains almost every entry on both lists: demand is falling for the predictable and rising for the judgment-heavy.

Technical skills rise because building and steering the machines takes judgment. Human skills rise because deciding what's worth doing — and catching what the machine gets wrong — takes even more of it. The routine in the middle is what erodes.

What to actually do with this

The pragmatic read isn't "technical vs. human" — it's both, weighted for durability. Enough technological literacy to work fluently alongside AI is close to non-negotiable now. But the skills that will still be paying off in a decade — the ones that make you better at directing any tool and worth promoting — are the human capabilities the WEF keeps ranking at the top.

The safest place to invest isn't the skill that's hottest today. It's the one that compounds instead of expiring — and those are almost all human.

Which is the practical case for knowing your own profile. If analytical thinking, creativity, resilience and social judgment are the durable currency of the AI era, the useful question is simple: how strong are yours, and which is your weak link?
